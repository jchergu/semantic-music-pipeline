# 3. State of the Art

This chapter surveys the scientific and technological landscape relevant to the proposed pipeline, organized around the three challenges introduced in Chapter 2: processing heterogeneous multimedia data, bridging the semantic gap between low-level features and human-understandable concepts, and supporting both batch and real-time processing within one framework.

## 3.1 Music Information Retrieval

Music Information Retrieval (MIR) is the foundational research area underlying the pipeline's feature-extraction stage. Librosa [8] offers a high-level Python API well suited to rapid prototyping, while Essentia [9], developed at the Music Technology Group of Universitat Pompeu Fabra, is a production-grade C++ library with Python bindings used in industry (e.g. Spotify/AcousticBrainz). AcousticID/Chromaprint provides the fingerprinting mechanism used to link ingested audio to MusicBrainz identifiers; AcousticBrainz itself, the community feature database keyed on those identifiers, was discontinued in 2022, leaving Chromaprint/AcoustID as the active linkage standard this pipeline still depends on for identity resolution, independently of whichever feature-extraction library computes the audio features themselves.

## 3.2 Bridging the Semantic Gap: Cross-Modal Representations

Recent work bridges the semantic gap using contrastive learning between modalities. CLAP (Contrastive Language-Audio Pretraining) [10] maps audio and text into a joint embedding space by training on paired audio-caption data, enabling zero-shot classification and semantic retrieval without a fixed label taxonomy, and is the model adopted in this thesis's enrichment layer. CLAP sits within a broader lineage of audio-to-joint-embedding methods rather than standing alone: Wav2CLIP [11] distills CLIP's image-text space onto audio directly, without requiring paired audio-text data of its own; MuLan [12] trains a music-specific joint embedding on 44 million audio-text pairs, at a scale and specificity closer to production music search than CLAP's general-purpose audio-event coverage; and AudioCLIP [13] extends CLIP to a tri-modal image-text-audio space rather than a bimodal audio-text one. CLAP was adopted over these alternatives primarily for its openly available pretrained checkpoints and its general-purpose, not music-specific, training distribution, which keeps the enrichment layer usable if the pipeline is later extended to non-music audio. CLaMP 2 [14] extends cross-modal MIR across 101 languages using large-language-model-generated captions, relevant to future multilingual metadata enrichment though not exercised on this thesis's English-language seed dataset. HTCL [15] bridges a semantic embedding space and a user-preference space specifically for recommendation through a two-stage contrastive objective, conceptually adjacent to this thesis's ranking design, which likewise combines a content-semantic signal (CLAP similarity) with graph-derived, preference-adjacent signals (genre-sibling, same-artist) rather than relying on either alone.

## 3.3 Vector Databases and Approximate Nearest-Neighbor Search

A joint embedding space is only useful at catalog scale if similarity search over it is fast enough to serve interactively, which for anything beyond a few thousand vectors rules out an exact, exhaustive comparison against every stored embedding. This is the approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) search problem, and the pipeline's choice of Milvus as its vector store is a choice among several credible systems and algorithms in this space, not the only one available. Milvus [16] is a purpose-built vector database offering multiple pluggable index types behind a client library and query API, which is what this thesis's Semantic API embeds directly rather than managing raw index files itself. FAISS [17], developed at Meta, is the closest point of comparison: a library rather than a managed database, offering similar index algorithms with GPU acceleration but without Milvus's persistence, multi-tenancy, or query-service layer. This is the distinction the architecture (Section 4.4) refers to when it specifies Milvus and not FAISS. HNSW [18] (Hierarchical Navigable Small World graphs) is, independently of either system, the graph-based ANN algorithm underlying the highest-recall index option in most modern vector databases, including as one of Milvus's own index types; it is cited here as the algorithmic foundation the system-level choice of Milvus sits on top of, not as an alternative to it. The prototype's own Milvus collection instead uses an IVF_FLAT index (Section 5.5.4), a choice made for indexing speed at this dataset's scale rather than for the highest attainable recall; Section 5.5.4's own repeatability analysis is careful to note that what was measured there is run-to-run repeatability against a static index, not recall against exhaustive search.

## 3.4 Knowledge Graphs for Music

Oramas et al. [6] established the foundational case for knowledge-graph-based music recommendation, showing improved interpretability and cold-start handling relative to purely collaborative approaches. This is a motivation directly reflected in this thesis's use of same-artist and genre-sibling graph signals as ranking features, already introduced from the recommendation-quality side in Section 1.1. Bertram, Dunkel and Hermoso build a comparable open-data music knowledge graph, from MusicBrainz-style sources, specifically to support recommendation via knowledge-graph embeddings [19]. This is the closest published precedent to this thesis's own Neo4j graph over MusicBrainz-linked metadata, though this thesis uses explicit graph traversal (genre-sibling, same-artist edges) as a ranking signal rather than a learned embedding of the graph itself. Gabbolini and Bridge take the traversal approach further, defining music similarity directly in terms of which paths through a knowledge graph are "interesting," and making the resulting similarity measure interpretable by construction [20]. This is a property this thesis's own signal-attribution evaluation (Section 5.5) shares in spirit, since every recommendation's genre-sibling or same-artist contribution is reconstructible from the graph rather than opaque. A separate line of work folds knowledge-graph structure into deep collaborative models rather than treating it as an independent signal: KGAT propagates attention-weighted embeddings along knowledge-graph paths jointly with the collaborative signal [21], RippleNet similarly propagates user preferences outward along knowledge-graph relations to expand a user's effective interaction set [22], and MKGCN extends this family specifically to a multi-modal music knowledge graph combining MFCCs, lyric sentiment, and audio sentiment as node features [23]. Liu, Yang and Cheng's MMSS_MKR framework takes a related multi-task approach, training a shared representation for the knowledge-graph-embedding and recommendation objectives jointly to address the same sparsity and cold-start problems Section 1.1 raises [24]. Huang, Liu and Chen's MuCoGraph applies a comparable graph-neural approach to auto-tagging rather than recommendation directly, combining preference, content-similarity, and tag-co-occurrence graphs to predict tags rather than rank tracks [25]. This is a reminder that graph-structured, multi-relational modeling in this space generalizes beyond recommendation to any task requiring semantic labels over a catalog, auto-tagging included, itself one of this thesis's sibling Layer-3 applications (Section 4.3). More broadly, semantics-driven approaches to data preparation are an active area beyond music specifically: the S-PIC4CHU project, undertaken in part at the University of Bologna's own Department of Computer Science and Engineering (DISI), proposes ontology- and knowledge-graph-enriched techniques for data cleaning, integration, and provenance across data science pipelines generally [26], [27]. This is a generalization of the same "semantics as an architectural layer, not a bolted-on feature" argument Section 1.1 makes for recommendation specifically.

Neo4j is adopted as the property-graph engine of choice, consistent with its widespread use in comparable systems; Protégé remains the standard tool for authoring the underlying OWL ontology (genres, moods, contexts) where a formal ontology layer is warranted. Apache Jena/RDF4J were evaluated as an RDF/SPARQL-standards-compliant alternative but were not carried into the final stack, in favor of the more directly queryable Neo4j/Cypher combination.

## 3.5 Stream Processing and Data Engineering

The pipeline's real-time capability rests on the industry-standard pairing of Apache Kafka for distributed event streaming and Apache Flink for stateful, low-latency stream computation with native support for windowed aggregation, essential for incremental feature extraction over live audio or behavioral event streams. Apache Spark remains the reference engine for the batch path, distributing large-scale feature extraction and knowledge-graph construction jobs; Apache Airflow orchestrates the resulting batch DAGs.

Architecturally, the pipeline follows a Kappa-inspired approach: batch as the primary paradigm, with streaming treated as a real-time extension rather than a duplicated parallel logic path. The terminology follows Kreps's original proposal [28], which argued that maintaining a separate, correctness-critical batch layer and speed layer over the same data (the Lambda architecture Marz and Warren formalize as a set of best practices [29]) forces every piece of business logic to be implemented and kept consistent twice. This thesis's own reuse of the batch ranking function (`platform/scoring/ranking.py`) directly in the streaming recommendation-refresh loop, described in Chapter 6, is a concrete instance of the discipline Kreps's argument implies: the streaming path calls the same scoring logic Chapter 5's batch path uses, rather than reimplementing it, avoiding exactly the dual-codebase drift a Lambda-style split would risk. Kafka itself, the log-centric substrate both paths read from and write to, follows the design Kreps, Narkhede and Rao first described as a distributed commit log for high-throughput log processing [30] rather than a conventional message queue. This distinction matters here because it is precisely the durable, replayable log that lets the platform's own raw-state consumer recover Redis session state after a flush by replaying the durable PostgreSQL event log, a recovery path a queue with destructive consumption would not support.

## 3.6 Multimodal Data Processing Beyond Audio

Album artwork can be processed through CLIP [31], the same contrastive image-text pretraining paradigm CLAP (Section 3.2) applies to audio, or captioned via BLIP-2 [32] or LLaVA for automatic semantic annotation; OpenCV and Pillow cover conventional image preprocessing. Music videos can be decoded and normalized with FFmpeg, with motion/energy patterns extracted via OpenPose/MediaPipe or self-supervised video transformers (VideoMAE, TimeSformer). Video is architecturally supported but offers marginal semantic gain over the audio-plus-artwork combination at catalog scale, and carries real availability/copyright constraints; it is therefore treated as an architectural extension point rather than part of the implemented prototype (Chapter 7 records this explicitly as future work, not an oversight). Textual metadata (lyrics, bios, tags) is covered by spaCy and HuggingFace Transformers, with Sentence-BERT providing efficient semantic-similarity embeddings for descriptive text.

## 3.7 Recommendation and Context Modeling

Collaborative filtering (matrix factorization such as ALS/SVD++) and content-based filtering remain the two baseline recommendation paradigms; production systems typically hybridize both. LightGCN [33] represents the current graph-convolutional state of the art for collaborative filtering. Context-Aware Recommender Systems [7] supply the foundational taxonomy for incorporating situational factors (time of day, activity, inferred user state), which motivates this thesis's context-aware framing of the Recommender Engine, even though the implemented 8.1 prototype uses a track-trigger model rather than full behavioral context (Chapter 5).

None of the paradigms above, however, addresses what use case 8.2 needs: a recommendation that updates continuously as a live session unfolds, rather than one computed once against a static context. Session-based recommendation research treats a session, not a long-lived user profile, as the unit a model reasons over. GRU4Rec models a session as a sequence consumed by a recurrent network, trained with a ranking loss suited to the many-short-sessions setting rather than the long, dense histories collaborative filtering assumes [34]. SR-GNN instead represents each session as a small graph over the items visited within it, capturing item transitions a purely sequential model can miss [35]. Neither, however, addresses the setting Chapter 6 evaluates: recommendations produced continuously by a running system rather than a single ranking computed once a session ends. Chang et al.'s formulation of streaming recommender systems as continuous-time processes, updated incrementally as events arrive rather than retrained in batch, is the closer formal match [36]. This thesis's own session-profile centroid, refreshed on a debounced schedule as behavioral events arrive (Chapter 6), instantiates the same continuous-update principle these methods formalize, using a considerably simpler mechanism than any of the three (a weighted vector centroid rather than a trained model), deliberately, given the dataset's scale (the compute constraint Section 1.2 states as an explicit objective) and the absence of the ground-truth relevance data any of the three would need to be trained or evaluated properly.

## 3.8 Evaluating Recommender Systems Without Ground Truth

A recurring methodological choice in Chapters 5 and 6 (reporting catalog coverage, intra-list diversity, and per-stage latency rather than an accuracy metric such as precision or recall at k) is itself grounded in recommender-systems evaluation research, not an ad hoc response to this dataset's lack of relevance labels. Herlocker, Konstan, Terveen and Riedl's survey of collaborative filtering evaluation establishes that prediction accuracy was never the only property worth measuring, cataloguing coverage, novelty, and serendipity as separate, independently reportable dimensions of recommendation quality from early in the field's history [37]. Kaminskas and Bridge's later survey formalizes exactly the "beyond-accuracy" objectives this thesis's own evaluation packs report: diversity (how dissimilar the items in one recommendation list are from each other), novelty, serendipity, and coverage (what fraction of the catalog a recommender is capable of surfacing at all), each with a defined, computable metric that does not require a ground-truth relevance judgment [38]. This thesis's own intra-list diversity metric (mean pairwise CLAP-embedding cosine distance within a top-10 list, Section 5.5) and catalog coverage metric (with its Gini coefficient, same section) are direct instances of the diversity and coverage families this survey defines, not novel metric designs of their own. What Chapters 5 and 6 do not attempt (precision, recall, or NDCG against relevance labels) is exactly the family Herlocker et al. treat as the default in 2004 and that this dataset simply cannot support, since no real users and no logged relevance judgments exist for it; reporting the beyond-accuracy family instead, and stating explicitly that no accuracy claim is made, follows the evaluation literature's own distinction between the two rather than working around a missing capability silently.

## 3.9 Reproducibility in Data-Intensive Pipelines

Machine-learning research has its own, well-documented reproducibility problem: Pineau et al.'s report from the NeurIPS 2019 Reproducibility Program finds that a majority of accepted papers omit the information (code, exact hyperparameters, dataset splits, hardware) a third party would need to reproduce their reported numbers, and proposes a checklist of what must be reported instead of left implicit [39]. This thesis's own evaluation packs (Sections 5.5 and 6.1) take a stricter, systems-level version of the same discipline: not just reporting what was measured, but measuring how repeatable the measurement itself is before trusting any number built on top of it. The batch pipeline's repeated-run noise floor (Section 5.5.4) (five independent runs of the real recommender, compared pairwise, with zero membership or score drift found across 41,100 matched track pairs) exists precisely so that a later claim ("the reconstruction script agrees with the real recommender") has a documented floor to be judged against, rather than an assumed one. This matters specifically because Milvus's ANN index is approximate, not exact (Section 3.3): a noise floor of exactly zero is an empirical finding about this system under these conditions, not a property Pineau et al.'s reproducibility checklist (written for the different failure mode of underspecified training runs) would have predicted in either direction for a retrieval system. The determinism metric Section 6.1.9 defines for the streaming pipeline, holding the same recommendation stable across repeated runs of an identical simulated session, generalizes the same discipline to a system with an additional source of nondeterminism the batch pipeline does not have: event-time window boundaries in a live stream.

A permissive, redistributable license and a scriptable, individually-addressable download path (rather than one large archive that must be fetched in full before any subsetting is possible) mattered most in practice for a reproducible ingestion run at the 200-500 track scale target. Jamendo satisfied both for this thesis's seed dataset; MusicBrainz supplies identity resolution rather than audio and is therefore never a candidate seed source in its own right; Freesound's role is deliberately different again, supplying short, clearly-licensed clips for use case 8.3's simulated live stream rather than catalog tracks. FMA was the closest runner-up and its rejection is detailed concretely, not just in the abstract, alongside the ingestion pipeline itself in Section 5.3.1: a disk-footprint argument specific to how FMA is distributed (per-split archives) rather than a licensing objection. Spotify and Last.fm were evaluated for their richer commercial metadata and scrobble/tag data respectively, but neither was pursued once Jamendo's API alone proved sufficient for the seed range targeted.

## 3.11 Summary and Positioning

Existing research addresses individual components of this workflow in isolation: MIR contributes feature extraction, cross-modal representation learning contributes joint embeddings, vector-database research contributes the retrieval layer those embeddings need at scale, knowledge-graph research contributes semantic structuring and interpretability, and stream-processing and session-based recommendation research separately contribute real-time ingestion and continuously updated ranking, but these areas are rarely integrated within a single, reusable pipeline that spans both batch and streaming regimes and multiple modalities. This thesis's contribution is precisely that integration, instantiated concretely through a demo Recommender Engine that consumes, but does not define, a generic, reusable Semantic API.
